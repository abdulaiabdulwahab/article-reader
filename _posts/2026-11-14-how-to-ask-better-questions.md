Good conversations often turn on a small moment: someone asks a question that opens a new way of looking at the problem. The question may be simple, but it helps people move beyond their first assumptions.

Asking better questions is less about sounding clever and more about being genuinely curious.

## Replace certainty with discovery

When we hear an idea we disagree with, it is tempting to respond immediately. A question can create room to understand what the other person means before evaluating their conclusion.

Instead of asking, “Why would you do that?” try “What led you to that choice?” The second version makes it easier to discuss reasons, constraints, and trade-offs. It invites information rather than defensiveness.

## Ask about the goal

Many disagreements begin because people are solving different problems without realizing it. Asking “What are we trying to achieve?” can clarify the shared objective and reveal whether a proposed solution actually matches it.

If the goal is unclear, a detailed debate over methods may not be useful yet. Establishing the outcome first can make the next steps more concrete.

## Make broad questions specific

“What should we do?” can feel overwhelming. A more specific question, such as “What is the first decision we need to make?” provides a starting point.

Specificity does not mean forcing a narrow answer. It means reducing ambiguity enough that someone can respond thoughtfully. You can then follow with broader questions about risks, alternatives, or assumptions.

## Leave room for silence

After asking a meaningful question, give the other person time to think. Silence is not necessarily a sign that the conversation has failed. Sometimes it means the person is considering something they have not put into words before.

You can also ask a gentle follow-up: “Could you tell me more about that?” It signals attention without pulling the discussion back toward yourself.

Better questions help us learn, collaborate, and make decisions with fewer hidden assumptions. The habit begins with a willingness to find out what you do not already know.
